BEHIND THE VISUALIZATION

One question.
Two perspectives.

How do daily highs vary across dates and places—and when did days in a particular temperature range occur?

The observations

This explorer uses the course-provided, transformed 2017 NOAA Global Historical Climatology Network daily weather file. We selected six recognizable airport stations with nearly complete daily maximum temperatures (TMAX, already in °F): Baltimore BWI, Chicago O’Hare, Denver International, Miami International, Phoenix Sky Harbor, and Seattle–Tacoma.

The source has 416,937 records and covers January 1–September 21, not a full year. Our common window ends September 17, the last valid high-temperature date at these six stations: 1,560 station-day records and 1,559 reported highs. Miami’s September 10 value is missing; it appears as a gap and is excluded from counts and averages. These airport observations are not citywide averages or a representative sample of national weather.

Why these two views?

The line chart reveals timing and day-to-day variation; the histogram reveals the frequency of temperatures in the selected period. Date and temperature use position, while day counts use bar height from zero. Fixed 10°F bins and shared temperature and count scales make station changes easier to compare. Counts describe observed days; percentages use the number of reported highs as their denominator.

We considered a bubble plot, a map, and a multi-station overlay. The bubble plot repeats the labs’ structure; a map adds geography without answering the time/distribution question; overlapping station lines add clutter. Two linked views keep exploration focused. The 90°F summary is an exploratory threshold, not an official heat-warning criterion.

Interactions and course connections

  • Category I: date brushing and date inputs filter the histogram; station selection updates both views; selecting a histogram bin links to its individual dates.
  • Category II: tooltips reveal daily values and bin counts; persistent selection highlights matching days. Clear selection and Reset all make exploration reversible.

We reuse the labs’ SVG, scales, axes, data joins, CSV loading, and event patterns. Labs 4–5 show one Gapminder bubble chart with a year slider and variable selectors. This project adds a time-series view, aggregation into a histogram, date-range brushing, and linking from bins back to observations. Lecture 5’s coordinated views and Lectures 3–4’s encoding principles guide the design.
