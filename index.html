Write Up

Dataset

We use the course-provided 2017 NOAA Global Historical Climatology Network daily file. The variable is daily maximum temperature (TMAX, already in °F) at six airport stations with nearly complete records: Baltimore BWI, Chicago O’Hare, Denver International, Miami International, Phoenix Sky Harbor, and Seattle–Tacoma.

The source has 416,937 records from January 1–September 21. Our window ends September 17, the last date with a valid high at these stations: 1,560 station-days and 1,559 reported highs. Miami’s September 10 value is missing and is left out of counts and averages. These are airport readings, not citywide or national averages.

Design decisions

The line chart shows when highs occur and how they change from day to day. The histogram shows how often temperatures fall in each range. Position encodes date and temperature; bar height from zero encodes day counts. Fixed 10°F bins and shared temperature and count scales make stations easier to compare. Percentages use the number of reported highs as the denominator.

We considered a bubble plot, a map, and one line chart with every station. A bubble plot would repeat the labs. A map would show location without showing change over time or the distribution. Overlapping lines would be hard to read, so we looked though the slides and found linked brushing to be a good way to show the relationship between the two views. The 90°F count is an arbitrary cutoff.

Interactions:

  • Brush the timeline, or set the date inputs, to filter the histogram.
  • Choose a station to update both views.
  • Select a histogram bin to highlight those days on the timeline.
  • Hover a day or bar to see its value or count.
  • Clear selection and Reset all undo those choices.

Development process

The two of us split the work between the data subset and the linked views. One person prepared the station subset, parsing, and histogram. The other built the timeline, brushing, layout, and we both wrote this write-up. We spent about 5-6 hours. Keeping the brush, date inputs, and histogram in sync, including Miami’s missing day, took the most time.
